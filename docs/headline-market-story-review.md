## Prologue: The Inheritance

Grandpa is found dead under mysterious circumstances inside The Sentinel, the small town's historical press building.

He leaves each of his five grandchildren $100,000 to invest over their lifetimes. Jonah also inherits Grandpa's gold Rolex and an old leather briefcase filled with newspaper clippings.

Impossibly, the clippings describe future events that have not happened yet. Jonah decides to use his inheritance and his knowledge of future headlines to maximize his investments over the next 20 years of his life.

Grandpa was found dead after midnight inside The Sentinel, and nobody knew what the thunderstorm had to do with it. He was at the bottom of the west stairs, coat soaked through, bark and leaves stuck to him, his leather briefcase scraped beside his hand. They could not make murder fit. They could not make natural causes fit. Accident was the only box wide enough for all the weird pieces.

Mr. Callahan read the will in a room that had too many clocks. Each grandchild got $100,000. The advice was plain: invest it well and thoughtfully. Eli looked ready to buy the whole market and leave it alone. Ruth liked gold because she could understand holding it. Max asked three questions that all sounded like the beginning of a bad loan.

He handed me Grandpa's gold Rolex in a little velvet box. It was set to 3:17, which felt specific in a way I did not enjoy. I wound it, and the second hand started moving like nothing strange had ever happened to anybody.

I opened the briefcase that night because I am not built for suspense. The top pages looked like Sentinel lobby display sheets, the kind they still clipped into the wall rails by the front desk. Then I saw dates that had not happened yet, stacked in order. Front pages from the future, or the most elaborate prank a dead man ever financed. I sat there until the coffee went cold and made the decision that starts this whole mess: if the future was going to leave headlines in my kitchen, I was going to use them to beat the market.

### Jonah's Final Journal

The last day did not feel like a finish line. It felt like the Sentinel had sprung a leak in every century at once. Mara was already in the basement with volunteers, passing archive boxes up the stairs while I did the heroic work of slipping on the same wet step twice.

The Front Page Hall finally gave up the trick. This was the same wall that had been feeding the town its big stories for twenty years, one clipping at a time. Every empty frame matched the brass-corner rubbings from the pages in the briefcase. Grandpa had not collected old newspapers at all. He had taken tomorrow's lobby pages during one borrowed minute and carried them back into yesterday.

The gold Rolex warmed on my wrist. The Sentinel clock clicked to 3:17, and the watch clicked with it. Then the west window bowed inward like the storm had put a shoulder to it. I saw Grandpa by the display rail, soaked coat, press badge cracked, eyes wide with the same surprise I felt. He shoved me sideways just as a tree came through the glass. I hit the floor hard. The trunk caught him across the head, and for one instant he looked both twenty years dead and right there in front of me.

Then he was gone. No speech, no tidy answer, no lesson wrapped in a bow. Just rain, broken glass, Mara yelling my name, and the horrible understanding that the accident in 1987 had been caused by him saving me in 2007.

At Ruth's kitchen table later, Eli, Ruth, Nora, Max, Mara, and I could all read the pages the same way. Not magic anymore. History. We passed the briefcase around like a family photo album with worse filing standards, and nobody knew whether to laugh, cry, or invoice the universe for emotional damages.
